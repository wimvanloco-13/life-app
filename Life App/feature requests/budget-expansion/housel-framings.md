# Housel Filter Framings

**Source**: Morgan Housel, *The Art of Spending Money* (Harriman House / Portfolio, 2025);
*The Psychology of Money* (Harriman House, 2020); Housel's Collaborative Fund essays.

**Purpose**: Verbatim framing copy for (1) the `LogBigPurchaseDialog` steps 2–4 (P2),
and (2) Library Item 6 "Before a big purchase: three filters" (P3).

**Verification note**: The frameworks below are well-grounded in Housel's prior published
work. The 2025 book's full text has not been read end-to-end; any specific phrasing from
that book should be re-verified before it appears as quoted copy in a Library item.

---

## Filter 1 — Inner Scorecard

**Dialog heading**: The Inner Scorecard

**Framing (2–3 sentences for the dialog)**:
Warren Buffett distinguishes between people who measure success by their own values (inner
scorecard) and those who measure it by others' opinions (outer scorecard). Almost all
durable happiness from spending comes from the inner scorecard. The outer scorecard is
unwinnable — there is always someone with more, a newer model, or a bigger house.

**Prompt shown to user**:
Is this purchase for you, or partly to signal something to others? Write down your honest answer — even a single sentence is enough.

---

## Filter 2 — Utility vs. Status

**Dialog heading**: Utility vs. Status

**Framing (2–3 sentences for the dialog)**:
Every significant purchase has two components: utility (how it functions in your life) and
status (how it signals something to others). Most expensive purchases are a mix of both.
The status portion is almost always the part that disappoints the fastest — because others
stop noticing within weeks, and so do you.

**Prompt shown to user**:
Which part of this purchase is utility, and which part is status? You don't have to choose one — just name both portions honestly.

---

## Filter 3 — The Six-Month Question

**Dialog heading**: In Six Months

**Framing (2–3 sentences for the dialog)**:
The brain adapts to any new baseline within roughly three months. Housel's rule: before a
large purchase, ask how you will feel about it in six months — not in six days. If the
honest answer is "I probably won't notice it," that is the answer.

**Prompt shown to user**:
How do you think you'll feel about this in six months? Will it still matter, or will it have become part of the background?

---

## Notes for implementation

- All three framings should appear in the same tone: calm, reflective, non-judgmental.
  The dialog is not trying to stop the user from buying — it is giving them a moment to
  think. The decision step is entirely theirs.
- The "prompt shown to user" lines are the textarea labels / placeholder guides.
  The framing paragraphs appear above the textarea as small body text.
- Library Item 6 ("Before a big purchase: three filters") will use the same frameworks
  but in a longer, more explanatory format with `what / why / how` fields.
